Finger Placement

First position — the most common starting pattern. Tap a dot to hear the pitch it should produce.

G string D string A string E string

Numbers are finger numbers (1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = pinky). "0" is an open string — no finger down.

This layout is often called Suzuki pattern 1: fingers 1 and 2 are a whole step apart, 2 and 3 are a whole step apart, and 3 and 4 are a half step apart (finger 4 sounds the same note as the next open string up — try matching them). Keep your fingers curved, hovering just above the string, and land on the pads of your fingertips rather than flat. Your thumb stays relaxed opposite roughly your 1st or 2nd finger, and your wrist stays straight rather than collapsing inward.

Left hand frame

  • Fingers stay rounded, never flat or straight
  • Each finger lands on its fleshy pad, close to the nail
  • Only one finger moves at a time — the others stay relaxed nearby
  • Thumb stays soft and still, roughly across from finger 1–2

Theory

The reading and vocabulary you need to get started — tap a section to open it.

Reading the staff
Clef, staff lines, ledger lines, and accidentals
▸

Violin music is written on a treble clef, five lines and four spaces. The open strings sit here:

Notice the open strings, low to high, spell G–D–A–E — a stack of perfect fifths. That's the shape of the whole instrument.

Ledger lines

When a note is too high or too low to fit on the five staff lines, short extra lines called ledger lines are added just for that note — that's what you see under G3 and above E5 in the diagram. The further above or below the staff, the more ledger lines are added.

Accidentals
  • Sharp (♯) — raises a note by a half step
  • Flat (♭) — lowers a note by a half step
  • Natural (♮) — cancels a previous sharp or flat, returning to the plain note

An accidental applies for the rest of that measure only, unless it's part of the key signature (see the next section).

Ties & slurs

Both are drawn as a curved line, but they mean different things: a tie connects two notes of the same pitch, joining them into one longer sound. A slur connects two or more different pitches, telling you to play them smoothly in one bow direction without a new stroke for each note.

Key signatures
Why some pieces have sharps written up front
▸

A key signature is a set of sharps or flats placed right after the clef, at the start of every line. It means those notes are sharped or flatted throughout the whole piece, so the composer doesn't have to mark an accidental every single time one appears.

G majorOne sharp: F♯. Every F in the piece is played as F♯ unless marked natural.
D majorTwo sharps: F♯ and C♯. Both are raised throughout.

These two keys are worth knowing early because they match the finger pattern you already use: the "whole-whole-half" first-position pattern from the Finger Placement tab naturally produces F♯ and C♯ wherever they occur — so you've been playing in these keys since week 3 without necessarily labeling it.

Rhythm & time signatures
Note values, rests, dotted notes, and counting
▸
Note values
  • Whole note (○) — hold for 4 counts
  • Half note (𝅗𝅥) — hold for 2 counts
  • Quarter note (♩) — hold for 1 count
  • Eighth note (♪) — hold for half a count, usually beamed in pairs
Rests

A rest is a measured silence — same durations as the notes above (whole, half, quarter, eighth rests), each with its own symbol. Counting through a rest out loud is just as important as counting through a note; it's easy to rush back in too early.

Dotted notes

A dot placed after a note adds half of that note's value again. A dotted quarter note (1 count + half a count = 1.5 counts) is very common, usually paired with a single eighth note to fill out the remaining half beat.

Time signatures

Most beginner pieces are in 4/4 time (four quarter-note beats per measure) or 3/4 time (three beats, a waltz feel). The top number tells you how many beats per measure; the bottom number tells you what kind of note counts as one beat.

Counting out loud

Say "1, 2, 3, 4" evenly for quarter notes in 4/4 time. For eighth notes, subdivide with "1-and, 2-and, 3-and, 4-and." Clapping or tapping a rhythm while counting out loud — before you touch the violin — makes it far easier to play accurately once the bow is involved.

Bowing vocabulary
Bow parts, strokes, and articulation marks
▸
Parts of the bow

The frog is the part you hold, at the thick end. The tip is the thin far end. The hair is what touches the string — it runs the length of the stick and is what actually produces the sound.

Bow direction
Down-bow (⊓)draw the bow from frog to tip, moving right
Up-bow (V)draw the bow from tip to frog, moving left
Playing style
Arcoplay with the bow (the default, unless marked otherwise)
Pizzicato (pizz.)pluck the string with a finger instead of bowing

Staccatoshort and detached, with a small stop between notes

A curved line drawn over a group of notes is a slur — it tells you to play that whole group in a single bow direction, without changing strokes between notes.

Left-hand finger numbers
How violinists number fingers, and what comes next
▸

Violinists don't number fingers the way pianists do. The thumb isn't counted; the index finger is "1," and so on:

  • 1 — index finger
  • 2 — middle finger
  • 3 — ring finger
  • 4 — pinky (sometimes skipped in favor of the next open string, which sounds the same pitch)

See the Finger Placement tab for exactly where each one lands, and the live checker there to test your intonation on each one.

Looking ahead

The pattern you're learning first is called first position, with a "high" 2nd finger (a whole step above finger 1). Many pieces in minor keys instead use a low 2nd finger (only a half step above finger 1) — your ear will start to notice the difference once you're comfortable with the basic pattern. Later still, shifting moves the whole hand frame up the fingerboard into new positions, opening up higher notes — that's a topic for well after these first twelve weeks.

How the violin is tuned
Fifths, standard pitch, pegs vs. fine tuners
▸

The four open strings — G, D, A, E — are each tuned a perfect fifth apart from their neighbor. That interval is why the violin's tuning has such a distinctive, open sound, and why a string that's slightly off will clash noticeably against the one next to it.

Standard pitch

The A string is tuned to 440 Hz by international standard (some professional orchestras tune slightly higher, around 442–444 Hz, but 440 Hz is the right target for learning). The Tuner tab in this app is built around A440.

Tuning by ear

Play two adjacent strings together and listen for a wavering, "beating" sound — as the strings get closer to a true fifth, the beating slows down and eventually disappears. That smooth, beat-free sound is what in tune sounds like.

Pegs vs. fine tuners

The large wooden pegs at the scroll make big pitch changes — used mainly for the thicker G and D strings, and turned carefully since they can slip. The small screws near the tailpiece are fine tuners, used for small adjustments, especially on the thinner A and E strings.

Ear training & listening
Small habits that make intonation and rhythm easier
▸

Reading notation gets you started, but your ear is what actually keeps you in tune and in time — and it improves with small, consistent habits, not extra hours of playing.

  • Before playing a new note, try singing or humming it first — if you can match it with your voice, your fingers have a target to aim for
  • Listen to a recording of any piece you're learning before you practice it, so your ear already knows what it should sound like
  • Record yourself occasionally and listen back — it's much easier to hear pitch and rhythm problems in playback than in the moment
  • When two notes sound "off" together, that clash is useful information, not just a mistake to feel bad about — it's telling you which direction to adjust
